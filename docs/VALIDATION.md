Case 7b was added with the atmosphere table, for the same reason. Cases 3b, 3c and 9 to 11 were added with the ABEP model, and cases 12 to 17 with the flight state machine. What they check is that the machine does what [FLIGHT_MODES.md](FLIGHT_MODES.md) says and that the energy and thermal books of a run agree with closed forms; they do not say that any threshold is right, because the thresholds are policy. Case 4, published results, is still to do; the Romano et al. curves are one source of the three it needs.

## Rules

- Every constant and every published number used in the code or in a test is cited here or in [`CONSTANTS.md`](CONSTANTS.md), with where it came from.
- Tolerances are written down before the comparison is run, not adjusted to make it pass.
- A tolerance must sit above the rounding noise of what it compares: a few unit roundoffs (1.1e-16 relative for a double) times the size of the terms in the calculation. Below that, a check passes or fails by the luck of the compiler and C library (finding 9).
- Two evaluations of the same expression in two places are not equal bit for bit: whether the compiler fuses `a * b + c` the same way in both is not promised. Compare them with a tolerance, and run the suite with fused multiply-add on, at every optimisation level and on more than one compiler, before trusting it (findings 14 and 17).
- If a case fails, the finding is recorded here whether or not it is later fixed.

## Findings

1. **J2 derivation: first attempt failed.** The test first evaluated the textbook formula for `q0` directly. That subtracts two numbers near 36.5 to leave about 7e-5, losing about five digits, so the 1e-15 tolerance asked for more than the formula can give. The tolerance was kept and the evaluation fixed: `q0` is now summed from its series in `e'`, which has no cancellation. The stored literal also carried only 13 digits and was off by 3e-16; it is now the correctly rounded double.
2. **Conservation of h_z: first attempt failed.** With a 10 s step and a 1e-10 relative tolerance over a day, h_z drifted by 2.2e-10 (measured afterwards: 6.9e-12 at a 5 s step and 7.4e-14 at 2 s, falling about 32x per halving). That is RK4's own amplitude error, about `(n * dt)^6 / 144` per step, and says nothing about J2; the tolerance had been estimated carelessly. The test now uses a 2 s step with the same tolerance. The other J2 tests keep a 10 s step, where the same error amounts to about 2e-9 over their 10-day runs, far below what they measure.
3. **Nodal-rate gap is second order in J2.** The propagated rate differs from the first-order formula by +0.43%. To check that this is a neglected higher-order term and not a wrong coefficient, the run was repeated with J2 scaled down: the gap was +0.4345% at J2, +0.2170% at J2/2, +0.1085% at J2/4 and +0.0436% at J2/10. It is proportional to J2, as a second-order term must be. Case 2 now asserts this.
4. **The `ussa1976` package disagrees with the printed standard, so the printed table was read.** The only implementation of the 1976 standard found, the `ussa1976` package (0.3.4 on PyPI), does not reproduce the report's table: it is higher by up to 6.7% (at 456 km), and by 6.5% at 400 km (2.984e-12 kg/m^3 against the printed 2.803e-12; that printed figure had been recalled from memory and is now checked against the report). Its tests do not compare it with the printed tables. A second package, `pyatmos`, could not be used here: its data download failed certificate verification, and verification was not switched off. The cause of the package's disagreement was not investigated. The source is the NOAA / NASA / USAF report *U.S. Standard Atmosphere, 1976* (NOAA-S/T 76-1562, NASA-TM-X-74335, [NTRS 19770009539](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19770009539)). Its PDF, a 17 MB scan with no usable text layer, was downloaded and Table I (100 to 1000 km) read by eye from page images. [ATMOSPHERE.md](ATMOSPHERE.md) says how that was checked; case 7 has the results.
5. **Primary source for the WGS 84 parameters not yet read.** See [`CONSTANTS.md`](CONSTANTS.md).
6. **Altitude must be measured above the ellipsoid.** Taking altitude as `|r|` minus the equatorial radius is wrong by up to 21.4 km at the poles and 13.1 km at 51.6 deg latitude, enough to move density by tens of percent. Measured along the geocentric radius above the WGS 84 ellipsoid, it is within 4.0 m of true geodetic height over latitudes 0 to 90 deg and heights of 200, 400 and 800 km. That worst-case figure came from a throwaway numerical comparison, and `tests/test_orbit.cpp` now repeats the comparison with a 5 m tolerance.

7. **Interpolation error: the estimate was low by about 2x.** Before running the test that leaves every second row out, the worst error for a 2 km gap was estimated at about 0.25% from the curvature of `ln(rho)` sampled at a few altitudes (about 5e-3 per km^2 near 120 km), and the tolerance was set to 0.5%. The measured worst case is 0.451% at 111 km: inside the tolerance, but only just. The curvature actually peaks near 111 km at 9.0e-3 per km^2 (from the second differences of the printed rows), which the few sampled altitudes had missed. Using that curvature, the estimate for a 2 km gap becomes 0.450%, matching the measurement, and for the shipped 1 km spacing 0.113% at 111 km, 0.020% from 150 to 200 km, 0.011% from 200 to 300 km, 0.014% from 300 to 500 km and 0.018% above. The error does not fall by a clean factor of 4 when the gap halves: with three of every four rows left out (a 4 km gap) the worst case is 1.378% at 114 km, 3.05 times the 2 km figure, because the curvature changes quickly there. So the shipped-table figures are estimates, not measurements. The tolerance was not changed.
8. **Mutation check of the table and its tests: one crash found.** In a scratch copy, the data file, the header and the interpolation were corrupted nine ways, one at a time: a digit changed in the header only; the same digit changed in header and data (a consistent misreading of one column); a slipped exponent; a dropped row; two adjacent densities swapped; a changed spot-value row; linear in place of log-linear interpolation; a change to the data file only; and an attempt to regenerate the header from a corrupted data file (the generator refuses, because the data fail its checks). Every corruption was caught by the C++ tests, the Python tool or both. The first run found a defect in the tests themselves: with a row dropped, the interpolation test's thinned table lost its top row, and an uncaught `std::out_of_range` killed the test program instead of reporting a failure. That loop now stops one row short, and the rerun reports ordinary failures. Only the consistent misreading of a single column (the second corruption) depends on the rho / rho_0 cross-check, which is why that check matters.
9. **A tolerance below the rounding noise passed on one toolchain and failed CI on two others.** The CI for the first pull request failed at the Test step on both Linux (GCC) and Windows (MSVC), and `main` stayed red after the merge ([run 44931817493](https://github.com/Overby-Industries/ion-propulsion-simulator/actions/runs/44931817493); exit code 8 from `ctest` on Linux, 1 on Windows). One check out of 2931 was responsible, in `tests/test_orbit.cpp`: after `circular_orbit_state` builds a state, `|r . v|` must be zero, since a circular orbit's velocity is perpendicular to its position, and the test allowed 1e-6 m^2/s. The product's scale is `|r||v|` = 5.3e10 m^2/s, so one unit roundoff of it is 5.8e-6 m^2/s: the tolerance sat below the noise. The tolerance had been set before running, as the rules require, but without asking how large the rounding noise of the quantity is. On the toolchain used until then (clang with the mingw-w64 C runtime) the state in the test happened to give -3.7e-7 and the check passed. With GCC and glibc it gives +9.5e-6, 1.6 unit roundoffs, as do Clang with glibc and the Windows C runtime (replayed through Python, which calls the same `sin` and `cos`). Over 685,152 circular orbits (300, 550 and 800 km; every 3 deg of inclination, 7 deg of node, 5 deg of argument of latitude) the worst `|r . v| / (|r||v|)` is 4.3e-16 (3.9 unit roundoffs) with glibc and with the Windows runtime, 4.0e-16 with mingw, and about two thirds of the orbits exceed 1e-6 m^2/s on all three. The tolerance was wrong, not the code. It is now relative: 1e-13 of `|r||v|`, which is 5.3e-3 m^2/s here, about 900 unit roundoffs and 230 times the worst noise found, the same relative scale as the length checks beside it, and still tight enough to catch a velocity 1e-13 rad away from perpendicular.

    How it was found: GitHub shows job logs only to signed-in users, so the failing check was not visible. It was reproduced by building the same commit with GCC 16.2 in a Linux (WSL) environment, which failed that one check and no other, with the same `ctest` exit code. The suite was then built 12 ways, with GCC 16.2 and Clang 21.1 on glibc at `-O0`, `-O2` and `-O3`, with fused multiply-add contraction off and on, and with `-march=native`. Before the fix that one check failed in all 12; after it, all 12 pass all 2931 checks, and so does a CI-style Release build and `ctest` with GCC. Skewing the velocity of `circular_orbit_state` by a relative 1e-9 or 1e-12 is still caught by the relaxed check (and by two others); 1e-14 is not, which is below what the check can resolve. In the GCC build every other `CHECK_NEAR` comparison uses at most 10% of its tolerance, except the geodetic-height one at 80%, which is deliberate: it is a modelling difference of up to 4.0 m against a 5 m tolerance (finding 6). **Confirmed afterwards:** that the Windows failure was this same check. It had been inferred from the Windows-runtime replay above; with the fix, CI passes on both Linux and Windows, for the pull request ([run 44937431073](https://github.com/Overby-Industries/ion-propulsion-simulator/actions/runs/44937431073)) and for the push to `main` ([run 44937494298](https://github.com/Overby-Industries/ion-propulsion-simulator/actions/runs/44937494298)).
10. **Published drag curve: first attempt failed.** The first comparison took the speed relative to the air to be the orbital speed (a non-rotating atmosphere), with a tolerance of 3% set beforehand, and failed by a constant factor at every altitude: the published curve is 0.886 times that drag, so the non-rotating drag is 12.9% above it (the test reports up to 13.1%). The paper defines `v_rel` as the speed relative to the atmosphere, so a co-rotating atmosphere was tried next, and it fits. The curve equals `1/2 * rho * (v - w R_E)^2 * C_D * A` to 0.1% at all 14 altitudes, with `v` the circular speed and `w R_E` Earth's surface speed (465 m/s). The simulator's own co-rotating form for an equatorial orbit, `v - w r`, is within 0.59%. The paper does not state this convention: it was inferred from the fit, and the same convention reproduces its mass-flow curves to 0.1% (case 3b). The test keeps the non-rotating variant as a check that it can tell the conventions apart.
11. **The standard atmosphere gives 6% to 33% more drag than the paper's.** In the 1976 standard atmosphere the simulator's drag for the same satellite is 1.06 to 1.33 times the paper's curve between 120 and 250 km. The ratio of densities, standard over the paper's NRLMSISE-00 case (F10.7 = 140, Ap = 15, at a date and place the paper does not give), is 1.34 at 120 km, 1.20 at 150 km, 1.06 at 200 km and 1.11 at 250 km. These are different atmospheres, so neither is wrong, but a power to hold altitude from this simulator is that much above what the paper's atmosphere would give, before any solar-activity variation. The test's band of 0.5 to 2 times is a sanity check, not a validation.
12. **Where the thrust-density range comes from.** In the literature review of Romano et al. (2018), the range traces to JAXA's air-breathing ion engine concept, which they give as 10 to 14 mN/kW at 170 km (frontal area 1.5 m^2, inlet area 0.48 m^2, capture efficiency up to 46%); their Table 1 spans 10 to 59 mN/kW across the studies they list. A web-search summary attributes the exact "10 to 13.7 mN/kW", and "4 to 5.59 kW" for full drag compensation at 170 km, to the IAC-17 conference version of that paper. That version could not be opened (the host refused the download), the review articles in the *Journal of Electric Propulsion* were behind a login, and the primary JAXA paper (Fujita 2004) has not been read. So the range is traced to a secondary source, and its conditions are those of one design concept, not a measurement of a thruster.
13. **State machine: the first version thrust itself into SAFE every orbit, and the energy balance found it.** In the first version COAST went back to THRUST as soon as the battery was at the stop level (0.20), so the restart level (0.50) applied only after CHARGE. After each shadow (300 Wh of a 1000 Wh battery) the satellite was at 0.31, thrust again at once, and entered the next shadow with too little in the battery: it fell through CHARGE into SAFE, where the load is shed to 100 W. In the 40-orbit run of case 14 the thrust duty was 0.534 against a closed form of 0.502, 3.2% out against a tolerance of 1%, and printing the mode timeline afterwards showed 6240 s in SAFE over the first 8 orbits, where there should have been none. The excess was the energy the SAFE load shedding saved. The tolerance was right and the code was wrong: the stop and restart levels are a hysteresis pair, and COAST had walked round it. Now the restart level applies after any stop (rule 6 in [FLIGHT_MODES.md](FLIGHT_MODES.md)), and a test of the machine on its own pins it down (a coasting satellite at 0.4999 stays COAST, at 0.50 thrusts). The transition-table test could not have found it: the table had been written from the same idea. It was the energy balance of a propagated orbit that disagreed. The note in [FLIGHT_MODES.md](FLIGHT_MODES.md) on sizing the battery for the shadow comes from this.
14. **A tolerance at the noise floor failed once fused multiply-add was on.** Case 16 first asked for 1e-6 m and 1e-9 m/s between the mission run and a direct propagation, on an estimate of the rounding noise ("about 1e-9 m") that had not been measured. Of nine builds (GCC and Clang at `-O0`, `-O2` and `-O3`; GCC with `-march=native`; GCC with FMA contraction off; GCC with the address and undefined-behaviour sanitizers) only `-O2 -march=native` failed, on those two checks out of 4540; the other eight passed. Measured afterwards, over nine settings: exactly 0 where the compiler does not fuse the operations differently in the two places, and 1.1e-6 to 2.6e-6 m and 1.3e-9 to 3.1e-9 m/s where it does, after 5000 steps. The tolerance sat at the noise: the same mistake as finding 9, found the same way. It is now 1e-3 m and 1e-6 m/s, 400 times the noise, and still catches a relative error of 1e-7 in the drag (drag moves the satellite by about 4e4 m over that run). The cumulative energy checks were also loosened from 1e-9 to 1e-6 Wh, since 21,000 terms of about a watt-hour can add up to 3e-9 Wh of rounding; they had passed in all nine builds.
15. **Three expectations in the first mission tests were wrong, and the code was right.** (a) The first sample of a run is CHARGE, not SAFE: the satellite starts in SAFE, but the first update already moves it up (one transition per step), so SAFE is never seen at the start. Four checks expected a SAFE sample there. (b) The density thresholds for the gating test were taken from the altitude of a circular orbit over the ellipsoid alone (300 to 313 km at 51.6 deg), but J2 makes the radius oscillate as well, and the orbit really runs through 288 to 306 km (density 1.7e-11 to 2.5e-11 kg/m^3): the thresholds were outside the range and gated nothing. They are now taken from a first run without gating. (c) A check assumed the speed relative to the co-rotating air is lower than the inertial speed; that holds for a prograde equatorial orbit, and for a polar one the air's 0.4 km/s is across the satellite's 7.8 km/s, so it is higher. In each case a failing check was followed back to the wrong expectation, not to the code, and the test was corrected.
16. **Mutation check of the state machine, the mission run and the thermal node: 33 of 33 caught.** In a scratch copy the three headers were broken 33 ways, one at a time, rebuilt and run each time. Twelve in the state machine: seven boundaries moved by one (`<` to `<=`, `>=` to `>`: the stop level, the SAFE entry and exit, the thermal limit, the fault temperature, the fault hold), the restart level removed from COAST, the CHARGE exit moved to the stop level, no density hysteresis on stopping, the eclipse policy ignored, an unavailable thruster not stopping the thrust, a new fault not restarting the hold count. Eighteen in the mission run: thrust reversed; all the electrical power becoming heat; a brownout not a fault; SAFE not shedding load; an array outage unseen by the machine, or ignored by the power; the thruster offered more than the battery holds, or the battery left out of what can be delivered; the per-orbit books counting the wrong mode or the sunlit time; a shortfall never noticed; the speed relative to the air ignoring Earth's rotation; the last step a full one; every step sampled; orbits counted at twice the period; drag 1% too strong; the intake seeing twice the density; heat taken from the thrust and not the power. Three in the thermal node: the time constant doubled, the heat capacity ignored without cooling, and the steady state multiplied by the conductance instead of divided. Every mutant made at least one check fail, and five were caught by exactly one check (thrust reversed, a brownout not a fault, the per-orbit thrust time, a shortfall never noticed, the heat capacity ignored), so those are the thinnest guards. The check was first meant to be run before the tests were finished; a review of which mutants would survive led to five additions made first (the per-orbit thrust time, the cap on the power offered to the thruster, the drag and relative speed in each sample, a run limited by the intake, and a last step shorter than the rest), so there is no record of what would have survived without them. After this the suite passed 4,564 checks with MSVC at `/W4` with no warnings. This paragraph first said that it also passed the nine GCC and Clang builds of finding 14; that was wrong, see finding 17.
17. **The Ubuntu job of the CI failed on the merge of the flight state machine, on a bit-exact comparison of two evaluations of one expression.** One check of 4564 failed, `tests/test_mission.cpp:210`: the relative speed in the first sample equal, bit for bit, to `norm(velocity_relative_to_air(sc.initial, true))` computed in the test ([run 45017192089](https://github.com/Overby-Industries/ion-propulsion-simulator/actions/runs/45017192089) on `main`; the pull request's own run, 45017111644, failed the same way; Windows passed both). Both sides are the same arithmetic, `v + w * y`, `v - w * x`, a sum of squares and a square root, evaluated once inside `run_mission` and once in the test, and they are equal only if the compiler fuses the multiply-adds the same way in both places, which nothing promises. **How it was found:** GitHub shows job logs only to signed-in users, so the log could not be read until the user signed in to the Browser pane; it showed the one check and the count. ## Known unknowns

- The thrust-density range is traced only to a secondary source (finding 12); the exact 13.7 and the primary JAXA paper are unread.
- The exhaust velocity has no sourced range: the thrust model needs it for the intake limit, and the only figure found (3.9 km/s) is an upper-limit estimate for one test device.
- Ram drag of the collected air is not modelled, and could change the sign of the net thrust ([ABEP_MODEL.md](ABEP_MODEL.md)).
- The standard atmosphere is a single moderate-solar-activity profile. Density tables for low and high solar activity, with a citable source, are still to be found, so that runs can bracket the answer ([ATMOSPHERE.md](ATMOSPHERE.md)).
- Real intake efficiency is unproven, so the capture-limited part of the thrust model has nothing to be validated against yet.
- The thresholds of the flight state machine are operating policy, and the coefficients of the thruster's thermal node (heat capacity, conductance, temperatures) have no source at all. Cases 12 to 17 check the logic and the books, not any of those numbers ([FLIGHT_MODES.md](FLIGHT_MODES.md)).
- The state machine cannot see an eclipse coming, so a battery too small for the shadow from the stop level drives it into SAFE every orbit (finding 13 is the first version of that). Whether a real policy should predict the shadow is open.
